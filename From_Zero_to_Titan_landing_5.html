THE PREMISE

THE RACE ISN'T THE STORY.

This chapter doesn't start with someone in shape. It starts on a couch, more than a year removed from cycling, further from fit than I'd like to admit. What I'm documenting is not six days in the desert — it's the eight months before that, the ones nobody films: the mornings I don't want to get up, the first ride that goes badly, a full Belgian winter of training in the cold and dark, the doubt about whether this was a mistake.

If I make it to the start line in April, chapter one is already a win — whatever the race itself decides. It's also just the beginning: three more chapters follow, each one harder than this.

YOU DON'T HAVE TO BE READY TO START. YOU HAVE TO START TO BECOME READY.
THE CHALLENGE

THE TITAN DESERT MOROCCO.

One of the toughest mountain bike stage races in the world: six days, roughly 600km through the Moroccan desert, with more than 6,000 meters of accumulated elevation gain across dunes, rocky trails and semi-desert terrain. Riders are largely self-sufficient, carrying their own repair kit and navigation, racing through extreme daytime heat and cold desert nights, under strict daily time cutoffs. It draws elite and amateur cyclists from all over the world — finishing it at all is already considered an achievement.

WHO'S TELLING THIS

NOT AN ATHLETE. SOMEONE WHO KEEPS STARTING OVER.

I'm Gonzalo Ayala. By trade I'm a field hockey coach and sports director, and founder of Veld, a field hockey app. Most of my life has been spent on a hockey pitch, not a bike. No background in endurance cycling. No team behind me. Until recently, no training routine at all.

That's the whole point. This isn't proof that a trained athlete can finish a hard race. It's an honest attempt to find out whether an ordinary person, starting from nowhere, can actually become someone capable of trying — and then doing it again, four times, each one harder than the last.
